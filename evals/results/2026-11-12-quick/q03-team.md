# Four-Day Work Weeks and Productivity: What the Published Evidence Shows

> What does the published evidence say about four-day work weeks and productivity?

The published evidence, drawn mostly from the 2022 UK pilot, suggests firms that cut hours to four days without cutting pay broadly kept revenue steady, but it does not show that the four-day week raises productivity [F2][F3]. Among the 23 firms that reported revenue, revenue rose only 1.4% on a size-weighted basis over the trial; the widely quoted 35% figure uses a different comparison against an earlier period [F3][F4]. Well-being results were stronger, with reported falls in burnout, sick days and resignations, though the trial report could not show the attrition and absence trends were statistically significant [F5][F6]. The evidence base has serious weaknesses: firms chose to take part, there was no control group, most outcomes were self-reported, and productivity itself was barely measured directly [F7][F11][F12][F15].

## Bottom line: output held steady, but productivity gains are unproven

The best available evidence suggests that companies in the UK pilot did not lose revenue after moving to a four-day week [F3]. That evidence falls short of showing that productivity improved [F11]. The pilot used a 100-80-100 model, in which workers got 100% pay for 80% of their previous hours in exchange for a commitment to maintain 100% productivity [F2]. On the trial's own terms, flat revenue on fewer hours is consistent with that commitment being met. However, one commentary notes that productivity itself was barely measured directly [F11]. Many productivity and revenue figures from these trials are self-reported by participating firms or trial organizers rather than independently audited [F15].

## The UK pilot: scale and design

The 2022 UK pilot involved 61 companies and around 2,900 workers, with pay maintained at 100% [F1]. Afterwards, 56 of the 61 companies continued with the four-day week [F1][F6]. One source reports that 18 of these adopted it permanently, though the quoted material does not directly confirm that figure [F6]. A critical reassessment notes that about 8% of UK trial participants discontinued the four-day week; this figure was only partly verified [F13].

## Revenue figures: 1.4% versus 35%

Two very different revenue numbers circulate, and they rest on different comparisons. Among the 23 companies that supplied revenue data, revenue stayed broadly the same over the trial, rising 1.4% on average when weighted by company size [F3]. A separate figure of +35% on average compares revenue with a comparable earlier six-month period, based on 24 companies [F4]. The 35% figure is the source of the widely quoted headline number, but it should not be read as a productivity gain caused by the trial [F4]. Both figures come from small subsets of the 61 participating firms [F1][F3][F4].

## Well-being, absence and retention

Results on worker well-being were more consistently positive than results on output. 71% of employees reported lower levels of burnout [F6]. One source reports a 65% reduction in sick days [F6]. This is roughly consistent with another account of sick and personal days falling from 2.0 to 0.7 per employee per month [F5]. Resignations reportedly fell from 2 to 0.8 per 100 employees [F5]. The trial report itself, however, says it is unable to show that these trends are statistically significant [F5]. A secondary blog reports that a 2025 Nature Human Behaviour study of 141 companies and 2,896 employees found lower burnout and better health, but that account was not checked against the paper itself [F8].

## Methodological weaknesses

**Self-selection.** The participating firms chose to join, which limits how far the results can be generalized [F7]. Michael Sanders of King's College London raised this criticism [F9]. A CIPD labour-market economist argued that the self-selecting sample means the trials fall short of robust evidence for policymakers [F11]. The trial researchers' own working paper reportedly acknowledges that companies self-select into the trials and calls this a source of selection bias [F10].

**No control group.** The fairest criticism of the UK trial is that it had no control group [F12]. One commentary describes the 2025 Nature Human Behaviour study by Fan, Schor, Kelly and Gu as a response to that criticism, though how fully it addresses the problem is not established in this evidence [F12]. More broadly, commentary notes that few trials have randomized control groups [F14].

**Measurement and bias.** Most outcomes were self-reported, and the organizers are open advocates of the policy [F11][F15]. A critical reassessment argues that Hawthorne and novelty effects may inflate short-term results [F13]. It also argues that productivity measurement in most trials is conceptually inadequate [F13].

**Narrow sector coverage.** There is little data from capital-intensive manufacturing, healthcare, or shift-based physical operations [F14]. Commentary also suggests the evidence skews toward white-collar work [F14].

## Limitations

- Nearly all the evidence concerns a single trial, the 2022 UK pilot. The question asked about published evidence generally, and other trials are not covered here.
- No direct, independently audited productivity measure is available; revenue is a proxy and was reported by only 23–24 of 61 firms.
- The 1.4% and 35% revenue figures use different comparison bases and different firm samples, so they cannot be reconciled into a single estimate.
- The 2025 Nature Human Behaviour study is known only through secondary commentary, not the paper itself, and its productivity findings, if any, are not described.
- Several details were only partly verified: the 18 permanent adopters, the 8% discontinuation rate, and the exact trial dates.
- Declines in sick days and resignations were not shown to be statistically significant.
- Self-selected firms, no control group, and advocacy by the organizers all make it hard to attribute outcomes to the four-day week itself or to generalize to other firms and sectors.
