# Sodium-ion vs LFP for grid storage: early commercial scale-up against an established incumbent

> How mature is sodium-ion battery technology for grid storage compared with lithium iron phosphate?

Sodium-ion batteries for grid storage are past the demonstration stage but still early in commercial use, while LFP dominates the market: it made up more than 90% of the roughly 307 GWh of storage installed worldwide in 2025 [F1]. Sodium-ion has moved from a 1 MWh pilot in 2021 to projects of 100 MWh and more in China [F4][F5][F6], and the first large manufacturing and supply commitments are only now arriving, including CATL's 60 GWh deal with HyperStrong and mass production planned for late 2026 [F9][F10][F11]. On cost, energy density and cycle life, sodium-ion still trails LFP, and lenders have no long-term fleet data to rely on [F12][F13][F15]. Analysts expect it to win specific niches, such as cold climates and hedging against lithium prices, rather than replace LFP; BNEF projects about 5% of storage additions by 2035 [F3][F16].

## Where each technology stands in the market

LFP is the clear incumbent. It accounted for over 90% of the 307 GWh of new storage capacity installed globally in 2025 [F1]. A BNEF analyst calls sodium-ion a less mature technology that may not yet have LFP's economies of scale [F2]. BNEF expects sodium-ion to make up about 5% of storage capacity additions by 2035 [F3]. One researcher-oriented comparison rates sodium-ion's grid track record as 'Early' and LFP's as 'Extensive' [F17].

## Deployment record: from pilots to 100 MWh-class plants

Hina Battery supported the first 1 MWh sodium-ion storage system in 2021 [F5]. A 10 MWh China Southern Power Grid station followed in May 2024 [F5]. China Datang's 100 MWh station in Hubei began operating on June 30, 2024 [F4], as phase one of a planned 100 MW/200 MWh project [F6]. Outside China, Peak Energy has deployed a passively cooled grid system in Colorado and agreed to supply Jupiter Power with 4.75 GWh from 2027 to 2030 [F7]. BYD grid-connected a 2.3 MWh demonstration unit in 2025 [F8]. These are real grid-connected assets, but they are tiny next to LFP's hundreds of GWh a year [F1].

## Manufacturing scale-up

Industrial scale is only now being reached. CATL says its Naxtra chemistry has reached GWh-level industrialization, with full mass production by the end of 2026 [F9]. In April 2026 CATL signed a 60 GWh, three-year stationary-storage supply deal with HyperStrong [F10]. It is also adding 40 GWh a year of capacity in Fujian for about $735 million [F11]. Most of this capacity is still planned rather than producing, which is a key sign of sodium-ion's relative immaturity.

## Technical and economic comparison

**Cost:** Citing Wood Mackenzie's 2025 data, one vendor puts average cell cost at $59/kWh for sodium-ion and $52/kWh for LFP, roughly a 13% premium for sodium-ion [F12]. Industry claims that sodium-ion will match LFP's cost remain unproven [F17].

**Energy density and cycle life:** Typical figures quoted are 100–160 Wh/kg and 1,000–3,000 cycles for sodium-ion, versus 160–200 Wh/kg and 4,000–8,000 cycles for LFP [F13]. Leading cells are closing the gap: CATL's Naxtra reaches up to 175 Wh/kg and keeps 90% of capacity at –40 °C [F14]. Volumetric density (energy per container footprint) still lags [F15].

**Where sodium-ion has an edge:** It performs better in cold weather [F14][F18] and uses no lithium. That is why analysts expect it to win where deep cold, heavy cycling or lithium-price risk matter most [F16].

## Bankability and outlook

For project finance, maturity means having operating data that lenders can underwrite. Sodium-ion lacks this, and its supply chain outside China is very thin [F15]. LFP therefore remains the default for most grid projects reaching financial close in 2026 [F16]. Overall, sodium-ion looks roughly where LFP storage was years ago: commercially viable, with real 100 MWh-class assets and GWh-scale orders [F4][F10], but with costs, lifetime and field reliability still to be proven at scale [F12][F17].
