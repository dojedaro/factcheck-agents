# Four-Day Work Weeks and Productivity: What the Published Evidence Shows

> What does the published evidence say about four-day work weeks and productivity?

The published trials mostly find that cutting hours to roughly a four-day week does not lower productivity. Most employers say output stayed the same or improved, while worker wellbeing clearly gets better [F1][F2][F11]. The strongest study is a 2025 Nature Human Behaviour paper with 141 organisations and a control group. It found better wellbeing and self-assessed job performance, but every outcome was self-reported and firms chose to take part [F5][F6][F7]. Hard financial or output data is thin: UK revenue figures came from only 23 of 61 firms [F4], and eye-catching numbers like Microsoft Japan's 40% are weak evidence [F8][F9]. The best reading is that productivity is maintained at volunteer firms that redesign how they work. Large economy-wide productivity gains have not been shown.

## The UK pilot (2022)

In the largest single-country pilot, a survey of 41 firms found that 46% said productivity stayed the same, 34% said it improved, and 15% said it improved significantly [F1]. Overall, employers said productivity was maintained, revenue stayed broadly the same, and sick days fell by 65% [F2]. Of the 61 firms, 56 continued the four-day week and 18 made it permanent [F3]. The financial evidence is limited, though. The 1.4% average revenue rise came from only 23 firms, fewer than two-fifths of participants [F4].

## The multi-country Nature Human Behaviour study (2025)

Fan and colleagues studied employees at 141 organisations in six countries and compared them with 562 employees at 12 companies that kept normal hours [F5]. Coverage reports higher job performance, satisfaction, and health with no pay cut [F6]. The gains were traced to less fatigue, better sleep, and greater self-assessed work ability [F8]. Before starting, firms spent about eight weeks restructuring work so they could keep output steady with 80% of their previous hours [F7]. That points to work redesign, such as cutting unnecessary meetings, as the mechanism, rather than the shorter week alone. The authors' own coverage warns that voluntary participation and self-reported outcomes could inflate the effects [F7].

## Iceland and other evidence

In Iceland's 2015–2019 public-sector trials, productivity stayed about the same or improved in some cases [F11]. However, those trials mostly cut hours to 35 or 36 a week rather than moving to four days [F12]. Other evidence comes mainly from employers' own reports: Microsoft Japan reported a 40% gain, and 70% of 10 Australian employers said productivity rose [F9]. Analysts call the Microsoft figure weak [F8]. Some popular summaries quote much larger, poorly sourced gains [F13].

## Where it may not work

A legal-academic review quotes a parliamentary committee that expected some front-line service industries to lose productivity, even though it said most evidence shows productivity can be maintained across the economy [F10]. Sectors with fixed staffing needs, like care, retail, or emergency services, are less able to compress the same work into fewer hours.

## Limitations

- Most trials (the UK pilot, the 4 Day Week Global trials, and the Nature Human Behaviour study) involved firms that chose to take part, which likely overstates effects for typical firms.
- Productivity is mostly measured through employer or employee perceptions rather than objective output. The revenue data covers a minority of firms.
- Very few studies have randomised control groups. The Nature Human Behaviour control group was small (12 companies).
- Interventions differ: some are full four-day weeks, others reduce hours by only 1–7 hours, and Iceland moved to 35–36 hours. That makes comparisons harder.
- Trial periods are short (about six months), so effects such as novelty or workers trying to keep the policy may fade over time.
- My search budget ran out before I could get sources specifically on criticisms by economists or the CIPD, or on studies with null or negative results. Some sources cited here are secondary summaries, not the original papers.
