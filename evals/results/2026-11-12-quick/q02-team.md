# Sodium-Ion vs. LFP for Grid Storage: A Maturity Assessment

> How mature is sodium-ion battery technology for grid storage compared with lithium iron phosphate?

Sodium-ion is still in early commercial deployment for grid storage and is clearly less mature than lithium iron phosphate (LFP). LFP has more than a decade of real-world grid performance data, while sodium-ion has essentially none [F13][F15]. Sodium-ion projects are growing, with the largest reported at 100 MW/200 MWh, but many installations are small or are minor parts of lithium-sodium hybrids [F2][F4][F8]. Sodium-ion's expected cost advantage has been undercut by falling LFP prices, and sources disagree on whether it is now cheaper, similar, or more expensive [F9][F10][F11]. Sodium-ion's main selling points are claimed temperature tolerance and simpler thermal management, but those figures come mostly from vendor specifications [F3][F6].

## Bottom line: early commercial, not yet bankable at LFP's level

The clearest gap between the two chemistries is field experience. LFP carries more than ten years of real-world battery storage performance data, while sodium-ion carries essentially none [F13]. Sodium-ion's main barriers are bankability and price: it has almost no field track record and barely any supply chain outside China [F15]. Commercial pressure has been severe, and several sodium-ion companies have failed under sustained LFP cost pressure [F14]. Manufacturers' expectations have cooled as LFP prices keep falling, and at least one analyst has lowered forecasts for how far sodium-ion will scale [F9].

## Deployment scale so far

Grid deployments exist, but they are modest next to the established LFP market. The largest sodium-ion project reported so far is 100 MW/200 MWh [F2].

In China:
- China Southern Power Grid commissioned the Baochi Energy Storage Station in Wenshan, Yunnan, around May 2025. It has been described as China's first large-scale hybrid lithium-sodium storage facility, though that characterization is only partly corroborated [F1].
- Baochi totals 200 MW/400 MWh, but only 40 MWh of that is sodium-ion [F2].
- The same utility deployed a 10 MWh sodium-ion battery in Guangxi as the first phase of a 100 MWh project [F8].

In the US, deployments are smaller still. Peak Energy reportedly switched on a 3.5 MWh sodium-ion battery in Colorado in 2025, described as the largest sodium-ion storage project in the country [F4].

The forward pipeline is larger:
- Peak Energy reportedly agreed to supply up to 4.75 GWh of sodium-ion batteries to Jupiter Power by 2030. The deal's value was reported as possibly more than $500 million, and the 4.75 GWh is a ceiling rather than a firm commitment [F5].
- BYD is reported to be building sodium-ion manufacturing at its Xuzhou plant, with a cited USD 1.4 billion investment and 30 GWh of planned annual capacity [F7]. This is a plan, not realized output.

## Cost competitiveness: contested and eroding

Sodium-ion's original pitch was lower cost than lithium-based chemistries, but that case has weakened.

- **Falling LFP prices:** BNEF's December 2025 survey reportedly found average stationary storage pack prices of $70/kWh, down 45% year on year, with the lowest observed LFP cell price at $36/kWh [F10].
- **Analysts who see no advantage:** Benchmark Mineral Intelligence's Matthew Bird is reported as saying sodium-ion's cost advantage over LFP has disappeared and that it is now more expensive [F11]. C&EN put sodium-ion at $60–100/kWh against under $50/kWh for LFP [F11].
- **A conflicting view:** the Fraunhofer CellEst model reportedly puts the two chemistries at similar cost [F11].

The weight of the cited evidence suggests sodium-ion currently has no clear cost edge, but the sources do not agree.

## Technical performance relative to LFP

**Energy density.** On IEA figures, sodium-ion cells reach at most 175 Wh/kg, short of LFP's 205 Wh/kg [F12]. This matters less for stationary storage than for vehicles, but it is still a gap.

**Temperature and efficiency.** Claimed specifications are attractive, but the evidence for them is only partial:
- One sodium-ion storage product is specified for -40°C to 60°C operation with 94% energy efficiency [F3].
- Peak Energy's NFPP system is reportedly rated at 95% DC round-trip efficiency, -40 to 55°C operation, and at least 20 years of service life [F3].

These are manufacturer ratings, not field-proven results.

**Thermal management and safety.** Peak Energy's system is passively cooled, with no active cooling or pumps [F6]. The company claims this removes over 85% of the root causes behind past storage failures, which is an unverified vendor claim [F6].

Without long-term operating data, none of these advantages can yet be weighed against LFP's established track record [F13].

## Limitations

- Several key data points carry only partial verification, including the Baochi facility description, Peak Energy's project size and deal value, BYD's capacity plans, BNEF price figures, and product performance specs. These should be treated as indicative.
- Cost comparisons conflict: Benchmark and C&EN indicate sodium-ion is more expensive than LFP, while Fraunhofer's model indicates rough parity [F11].
- Performance figures for sodium-ion (efficiency, temperature range, 20+ year life, reduced failure causes) come largely from vendor specifications or claims, not independent field data [F3][F6].
- The evidence gives no data on sodium-ion cycle life or degradation in the field, nor on total installed global sodium-ion grid capacity, which limits any quantitative maturity comparison.
- Evidence on LFP is limited to price, energy density, and track-record length. Its own deployment scale and performance are not directly documented here.
- Several sources are market-research or trade-press summaries (e.g., IndexBox, market reports), not primary data.
